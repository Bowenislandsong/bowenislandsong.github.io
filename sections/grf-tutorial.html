Step 1

What is ground reaction force?

Ground reaction force (GRF) is the force the ground applies to the body while the body is touching the ground. If the foot pushes down and backward on the ground, the ground pushes back up and forward on the foot.

GRF is a 3D vector. A lab usually resolves it into vertical force \(F_z\), anterior-posterior force \(F_x\), and medial-lateral force \(F_y\). The vector acts at the center of pressure (CoP), the point on the contact surface where the resultant force can be represented.

GRF exists only during contact for the foot being analyzed. During swing, the GRF for that foot should be zero. In double support, each foot has its own external load; a single total force from both feet is not enough to compute left and right joint moments cleanly.

Step 2

How do force plates measure GRF?

A modern force plate is a calibrated multi-axis sensor. It typically reports six signals in the plate coordinate frame: three forces \(F_x, F_y, F_z\) and three moments \(M_x, M_y, M_z\). From those values, the software computes CoP and the free moment about the vertical axis. For inverse dynamics, the useful representation is force + point of application + free moment.

Force plate outputs

Forces: \(F_x, F_y, F_z\), usually anterior-posterior, medial-lateral, and vertical after coordinate mapping.

Moments: \(M_x, M_y, M_z\), measured about the force-plate origin, not automatically about the ankle, knee, or hip.

CoP: the ground point where the resultant force can be applied with the same net effect, after accounting for plate geometry.

Free moment: the torsional moment about the vertical axis at the CoP. It is often smaller than force components but useful in turning and foot-rotation studies.

Typical CoP form

\(CoP_x \approx \frac{-M_y + zF_x}{F_z}\), \(CoP_y \approx \frac{M_x + zF_y}{F_z}\)

Exact signs depend on the plate coordinate system and the manufacturer's origin convention.

Instrument Best for Main limitation
Overground force plate Precise 3D force, CoP, and free moment for a clean foot strike. Short capture volume; targeting the plate can alter natural gait.
Instrumented treadmill Many consecutive gait cycles, often with separate left/right belts. Treadmill mechanics and speed control can differ from overground walking or running.
Pressure insole Contact timing, plantar pressure distribution, and approximate foot loading patterns. Usually not a full 3D GRF system; shear force and free moment are limited or absent.

Step 3

How to read GRF curves

Vertical GRF: walking commonly shows two peaks with a valley between them. The first peak is weight acceptance after heel strike; the second is push-off. Running waveforms differ by speed and foot strike pattern.

Anterior-posterior GRF: early stance is usually braking, late stance is propulsion. The sign depends on the lab coordinate system.

Medial-lateral GRF: usually smaller and more sensitive to foot placement, balance, and sign convention.

Impulse: area under a force-time curve. Vertical impulse relates to change in vertical momentum; AP braking and propulsive impulses describe speed regulation.

Step 4

What can IMUs estimate?

IMUs measure acceleration and angular velocity. With sensor fusion they estimate orientation. They do not directly sense external force. IMU-based GRF is therefore an indirect estimate built from a physical model, a learned mapping, or both, and should be validated against force plates.

Good IMU targets

Gait events, segment orientation, cadence, stance time, contact time, vertical acceleration features, and single-foot contact segmentation.

Harder targets

Full 3D GRF, shear force, CoP, free moment, left/right force split in double support, and generalization across people, speeds, shoes, and tasks.

Validation metrics

RMSE or nRMSE, MAE, correlation, peak error, timing error, impulse error, and task-specific clinical or sport variables.

Step 5

What is a joint moment?

A moment is the turning effect of a force about a joint. In vector form, \( \mathbf{M} = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F} \). The lever arm is the perpendicular distance from the joint center to the line of action of the force.

An external moment is the rotational tendency caused by external loads such as GRF, gravity, and inertia. An internal moment is the net biological moment that balances or produces movement, commonly attributed to muscles and passive tissues as a combined result.

A net joint moment is not an individual muscle force. Co-contraction, biarticular muscles, ligaments, joint contact, and passive tissue behavior can produce the same net value in different ways.

Always ask: Is the plotted moment internal or external? Which coordinate frame? Which sign convention? Normalized to body mass or not?

Step 6

How GRF creates ankle, knee, and hip moment demands

Ankle

Plantarflexion / dorsiflexion moment

The ankle moment is highly sensitive to CoP progression under the foot. During late stance, CoP moves forward and the plantarflexors usually generate a large internal plantarflexion moment for push-off.

Needed inputs: foot pose and acceleration, ankle joint center, foot mass/inertia, GRF, CoP, free moment if included.

Knee

Flexion / extension and adduction moments

Sagittal knee moments depend on shank dynamics and the ankle reaction terms propagated upward. Frontal-plane knee adduction moments require a 3D model and are often used as external loading indicators, not direct compartment contact force.

Needed inputs: shank pose and acceleration, knee and ankle centers, shank inertial properties, ankle reaction force/moment from the foot solution.

Hip

Flexion / extension, abduction / adduction, rotation

Hip moments are computed from thigh dynamics plus knee reaction terms. Hip abduction and rotation moments are especially sensitive to pelvis tracking, hip joint center estimation, and coordinate-frame definitions.

Needed inputs: thigh and pelvis kinematics, hip and knee centers, thigh inertial properties, knee reaction force/moment from the shank solution.

Step 7

How inverse dynamics computes joint moments

Inverse dynamics solves the Newton-Euler equations backward from measured motion and measured or modeled external loads. For lower-limb stance, the common teaching order is distal-to-proximal: solve the foot, then shank, then thigh.

01

Define coordinate frames

Lab, force plate, sensor, segment, and joint coordinate systems must be mapped consistently before computing moments.

02

Synchronize and filter

Noise is amplified when positions are differentiated to velocity and acceleration, so filtering choices materially affect moments.

03

Assign foot contacts

Apply each force to the correct foot. Wrong plate assignment often creates implausible pelvis residuals or joint moments.

Core equations

Linear dynamics: \(\sum \mathbf{F} = m\mathbf{a}_{COM}\)

Angular dynamics: \(\sum \mathbf{M}_{COM} = \mathbf{I}\boldsymbol{\alpha} + \boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{I}\boldsymbol{\omega}\)

Moment from GRF about joint \(j\): \(\mathbf{M}_{GRF,j} = \mathbf{r}_{CoP/j} \times \mathbf{F}_{GRF}\)

Net joint moment: the residual rotational quantity needed to make the segment equations match the measured accelerations and external loads.

Step 8

A practical checklist for trustworthy results

Before collection

  • • Record subject mass, height, limb lengths, footwear, task, and speed.
  • • Document coordinate frames and positive directions.
  • • Calibrate force plates and zero them before trials.
  • • Place markers or IMUs so joint centers and segment frames can be estimated.

During processing

  • • Align force and kinematic timestamps.
  • • Filter kinematics before differentiation and report cutoffs.
  • • Check that CoP stays under the contacting foot during stance.
  • • Confirm GRF goes to zero during swing for that foot.

When reporting

  • • State whether moments are internal or external.
  • • Report units: N, body weight, N*m, or N*m/kg.
  • • Include sign conventions and coordinate systems.
  • • Compare waveform shapes, peaks, timing, and impulses.

Red flags

  • • Joint moments computed from force magnitude without lever arms.
  • • IMU estimates described as direct GRF measurements.
  • • Left/right force mixing during double support.
  • • Conclusions about individual muscles from net joint moments alone.
