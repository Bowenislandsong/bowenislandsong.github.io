Biomechanics Tutorial

Ground reaction force and joint moments, from first principles to measurement.

This tutorial explains what GRF is, how researchers measure it, what joint moments mean, and how ankle, knee, and hip moments are computed from movement data.

The central idea is simple: GRF is an external force you can measure at the ground. Joint moments are usually not measured directly; they are estimated with inverse dynamics from body motion, body-segment properties, and external loads such as GRF and center of pressure.

Step 1

What is ground reaction force?

Ground reaction force (GRF) is the force the ground applies back to the body while the body is in contact with it. It is the reaction side of the foot pushing on the ground.

GRF is a vector, not a single number. In gait and sports biomechanics it is commonly decomposed into vertical, anterior-posterior, and medial-lateral components. The vertical component is usually the largest during walking and running.

The force also has a point of application: the center of pressure (CoP). For joint moments, CoP is critical because moment depends on both the force magnitude and the force's lever arm relative to a joint center.

A force plate reports the resultant force vector and where it acts on the plate. The CoP-to-joint distance is the lever arm used in moment calculations.

Step 2

How do you measure GRF?

The direct measurement is a force plate or instrumented treadmill. These systems measure the interaction between the foot and ground, usually reporting three force components, moments at the plate, and CoP. Pressure insoles can estimate plantar pressure and CoP-like quantities, but they are not the same as a calibrated 3D force plate.

01

Zero and calibrate

Remove baseline offsets before the trial. Confirm plate axes and units: force in N, CoP in m or mm, moments in N*m.

02

Synchronize sensors

Sync force data with motion capture, video, or IMUs. Small timing errors can shift peak forces and joint moments.

03

Detect stance

Use a vertical-force threshold to identify foot contact. Outside stance, GRF should be zero for that foot.

04

Report clearly

Normalize force to body weight when comparing people. Report axes, filtering, footwear, task, speed, and contact assignment.

Instrument What it gives you Main caution
Force plate 3D force, CoP, and plate moments for overground steps, jumps, landings, and balance. Foot must land cleanly on the plate; partial foot strikes corrupt CoP and force assignment.
Instrumented treadmill Continuous left/right GRF during many gait cycles, often with split belts. Treadmill gait can differ from overground gait; belt and coordinate conventions must be documented.
Pressure insole Foot pressure distribution, contact timing, approximate load distribution, and CoP trajectory. It is generally not a full 3D GRF measurement; shear forces and calibration drift are hard.
IMU system Segment acceleration, angular velocity, orientation estimates, and contact-event features. An IMU estimates GRF through a model or machine-learning mapping; it does not directly measure external force.

Step 3

What can IMUs estimate?

Good at

  • • Gait events such as heel strike and toe-off.
  • • Segment orientation, angular velocity, and acceleration.
  • • Features for estimating vertical GRF when trained and validated against force plates.

Not directly observed

  • • CoP under the foot.
  • • Foot-ground shear force without additional assumptions.
  • • Left/right force split from one body-mounted sensor during double support.

Useful approximation

For whole-body vertical force, a simplified relation is \(F_z \approx m(a_{COM,z} + g)\). This requires a reliable center-of-mass acceleration estimate and does not by itself provide CoP or joint moments.

Language matters: force-plate GRF is measured. IMU-derived GRF is estimated or predicted unless the IMU is part of a calibrated system with explicit force-sensing hardware.

Step 4

What is a joint moment?

A moment is the rotational effect of a force about an axis. For a force acting at a distance from a joint center, the basic vector relation is \( \mathbf{M} = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F} \), where \( \mathbf{r} \) is the lever arm from the joint to the point of force application.

A net joint moment is the combined effect needed at the joint to explain the observed segment motion. It is not the force of one muscle. Many muscles, ligaments, contact forces, and passive structures can contribute to the same net moment.

Most gait labs report internal joint moments: the net muscular/passive moment that balances the external moment from GRF, gravity, and segment inertia. Some software reports external moments instead, so signs must be defined.

Coordinate axes you will see

Sagittal plane: flexion/extension moments. Common for ankle plantarflexion/dorsiflexion, knee flexion/extension, and hip flexion/extension.

Frontal plane: abduction/adduction moments, including knee adduction moment and hip abduction moment.

Transverse plane: internal/external rotation moments. These need reliable 3D kinematics and careful marker/sensor alignment.

Step 5

Joint moments by lower-limb joint

The same physics applies at every joint, but the required inputs and interpretation differ. A full 3D analysis can report moments about each joint coordinate. A first tutorial usually begins with sagittal-plane ankle, knee, and hip moments because they are central to walking, running, jumping, and sit-to-stand tasks.

Ankle

Plantarflexion / dorsiflexion moment

The ankle moment is strongly affected by where the CoP sits under the foot relative to the ankle joint center. During push-off, the net internal plantarflexor moment reflects the demand commonly met by the calf muscle group. To compute it, you need foot kinematics, foot inertial parameters, GRF, CoP, and the ankle joint center.

Knee

Flexion / extension and adduction moments

The knee moment depends on the shank's motion and on forces/moments transmitted from the foot-ankle segment. Sagittal-plane knee flexion/extension moments are common in gait and landing studies. Frontal-plane knee adduction moment is also widely reported, but it requires reliable 3D alignment and should not be inferred from a sagittal-only model.

Hip

Flexion / extension, abduction / adduction, rotation

The hip moment is computed from thigh and pelvis kinematics plus the reaction terms propagated from the knee. Hip flexion/extension is central in sagittal analyses. Hip abduction/adduction and rotation moments require 3D kinematics, a pelvis coordinate system, and careful sign conventions.

Step 6

How joint moments are computed

Inverse dynamics works backward from measured motion and external loads. In the lower limb, calculations usually proceed distal-to-proximal: foot to shank to thigh to pelvis. Each segment is treated as a rigid body with known mass, center of mass, and moment of inertia.

01

Collect kinematics

Measure joint centers, segment poses, velocities, and accelerations from motion capture or validated IMU models.

02

Collect external loads

Use measured GRF, CoP, and free moment when available. Without external loads, lower-limb joint moments become underconstrained.

03

Filter consistently

Differentiation amplifies noise, so kinematics are commonly low-pass filtered. Report cutoffs and filtering order.

04

Solve foot first

GRF and CoP define the external loading on the foot. Segment equations solve ankle reaction force and ankle moment.

05

Propagate upward

Use ankle terms to solve shank dynamics and knee moment. Use knee terms to solve thigh dynamics and hip moment.

06

Normalize and interpret

Report moments in N*m and often N*m/kg. Compare waveform shape, peak magnitude, and timing to reference data.

Core equations

Linear dynamics: \(\sum \mathbf{F} = m\mathbf{a}_{COM}\)

Angular dynamics about joint \(j\): \(\sum \mathbf{M}_j = \mathbf{I}\boldsymbol{\alpha} + \boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{I}\boldsymbol{\omega}\)

Moment from GRF: \(\mathbf{M}_{GRF,j} = \mathbf{r}_{CoP/j} \times \mathbf{F}_{GRF}\)

Sagittal first pass: use the out-of-plane component of \(\mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F}\), then keep the same flexion/extension sign convention for every trial.

Step 7

Validation and factual guardrails

Validate signals

  • • Compare estimated GRF against force-plate GRF on synchronized trials.
  • • Report RMSE/MAE, correlation, peak error, loading-rate error, and timing error.
  • • Check left/right foot assignment and CoP location for every contact.
  • • Inspect whether GRF goes to zero during swing.

Avoid common mistakes

  • • Do not mix coordinate systems without transforming vectors into one frame.
  • • Do not compute joint moments from force magnitude alone; the lever arm matters.
  • • Do not call a net joint moment a single muscle torque.
  • • Do not compare moments unless body-mass normalization and sign conventions match.
