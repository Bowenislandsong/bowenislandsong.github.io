Bowen Song · USC Viterbi

Research engineer who turns difficult machine learning ideas into systems that stay useful under real constraints.

My strongest work sits where research, evaluation, and implementation all have to be good at once. I work on machine learning problems where the data are noisy, the users are heterogeneous, the compute budget matters, and the result still has to be reliable enough for someone to act on.

That pattern shows up across my record. In my work on wearable biomechanics, I introduced SVD Embedding Regression, a low-rank method that matched or beat heavier LSTM baselines for ground-reaction-force estimation while remaining interpretable and fast enough for edge deployment. In systems work, I built a formal model of hybrid cloud federation under QoS and incentive constraints using Shapley value, and earlier I designed RCDS, a reconciliation algorithm that scales linearly with edit distance and beats rsync on a substantial class of realistic workloads.

I reinforced that research profile in industry through ranking research at eBay, streaming infrastructure at eBay, and platform engineering on OpenShift at Red Hat. The common thread is that I do not want to stop at an idea that sounds smart; I want the result to be measurable, deployable, and strong enough that another team would trust it.

Why Me

What is unusual about the profile.

Research instinct

I look for the simplification that creates leverage.

My work usually starts with an ambiguous question, moves through data and modeling choices, and ends with a result that is reproducible enough to trust and iterate on. In my best work, that means identifying when the field is over-paying for complexity and proving that a simpler approach can preserve or improve the result.

That matters because high-end research organizations do not only need clever ideas. They need people who can turn those ideas into legible, measurable, extensible systems so the next experiment starts from a stronger place.

Breadth with a through-line

The topics are broad, but the technical habit is consistent.

My papers span wearable ML, federated and personalized learning, cloud federation, and distributed reconciliation, and my industry work spans ranking, streaming infrastructure, and Kubernetes platforms. That range is not random. It reflects one technical preference: work on systems that have to stay correct and useful when constraints start to dominate.

That is part of why I translate well to ambitious AI teams. The surface area changes, but the underlying work of reasoning under uncertainty, evaluating honestly, and shipping robust systems does not.

Systems credibility

I do not treat infrastructure as somebody else’s problem.

I have worked on ranking systems, streaming analytics, Kubernetes platforms, and reliability tooling, which means I think about scale, cost, debugging, observability, and operational safety early. I do not see those as downstream concerns; I see them as part of technical quality from the start.

That is often the difference between research that looks promising in isolation and research that a team can actually build on, integrate, and ship with confidence.

Force multiplier

I make the work easier for other strong people to build on.

My best work happens when a team expects rigor, speed, and ownership at the same time. I like environments where people care about ablations, strong writing, useful code review, and the discipline to revise a weak idea instead of over-selling it.

I also like being the person who helps keep that bar intact: mentoring students, tightening experiments, clarifying the story, packaging ideas into code, and making sure the implementation is as defensible as the claim.

USC · 2020 to present

Personalized AI for Health

Led end-to-end research on real-time biomechanical estimation from wearable signals in the QED Research Group, with an emphasis on interpretable models, personalization, and deployable inference. The work sits at the intersection of modeling, sensing, and systems because none of those pieces can be weak if the result is supposed to matter outside a lab study.

The work led to first-author publications and to methods like SVD Embedding Regression, which showed that lightweight models could stay competitive while reducing inference cost dramatically. This is where I learned to treat data pipelines, model design, evaluation, and deployment constraints as one problem rather than four separate ones.

It also shaped the kind of research engineer I am now: someone who wants the paper to be strong, but also wants the model to be fast enough, inspectable enough, and stable enough that another team could realistically build on it.

eBay · 2024

Core Ranking Research Intern

Worked on mixture-of-experts ranking models, large-scale evaluation, and training workflows that reflected production ranking constraints instead of toy offline settings at eBay. Ranking is a great training ground for research engineering because every gain has to justify itself against strong baselines, business constraints, and heterogeneous traffic.

That experience sharpened my instincts for large-scale experimentation, multi-objective optimization, and the reality that a good model has to earn its place against strong baselines, traffic heterogeneity, and operational limits.

It made me better at the kind of judgment ambitious AI teams need: knowing when a modeling idea is truly better, when the evaluation is lying, and when the system around the model is the real bottleneck.

eBay · 2023

Infrastructure Intern

Owned a Kafka-to-ClickHouse streaming pipeline for a multi-datacenter analytics stack with strong attention to exactly-once behavior, schema evolution, and operability at eBay. It was not glamorous work, but it was the kind of work that determines whether a data-driven system can be trusted day after day.

It gave me direct responsibility for a production-flavored data system where reliability mattered more than cleverness, and it reinforced that I can contribute in the systems layer, not only the model layer.

That experience still shows up in how I work now: I care about interfaces, observability, failure recovery, and the boring details that separate a convincing demo from a dependable system.

Red Hat · 2019 to 2020

Software Engineer, OpenShift

Contributed to Operator Lifecycle Manager, Helm integration, dependency management, and flaky-test detection across the OpenShift Kubernetes platform at Red Hat. OpenShift taught me how mature platform teams manage complexity without accepting fragility as the price of scale.

That work gave me strong instincts around maintainability, versioning, release safety, and operational correctness, which still shape how I approach research code and infrastructure today.

It is also one reason I care so much about software quality in research settings: the best ideas compound faster when the codebase is something other people can trust.
